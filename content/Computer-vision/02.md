## Part 0: Review — The Camera Exposure Plane (Slide 2)

Slide 2 establishes the physical trade-offs in camera acquisition through a two-axis exposure coordinate plane:

- **Vertical Axis (Aperture / F-number):** Ranges from **Large Aperture** ($f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6$) at the top to **Small Aperture** ($f/8, f/11, f/16, f/22, f/32$) at the bottom.
- **Horizontal Axis (Shutter Speed):** Ranges from **Fast Shutter** ($1/1000\text{ s}, 1/500\text{ s}, 1/250\text{ s}, 1/125\text{ s}, 1/60\text{ s}$) on the left to **Slow Shutter** ($1/30\text{ s}, 1/15\text{ s}, 1/8\text{ s}, 1/4\text{ s}, 1/2\text{ s}, 1''$) on the right.

```
                         Large Aperture (f/2)
                         [Shallow DOF / More Light]
                                   ^
                     Quadrant A    |    Quadrant B
                   (Fast & Wide)   |   (Slow & Wide)
                                   |   ===> BRIGHT
       Fast Shutter <--------------+--------------> Slow Shutter
       [Frozen Motion]             |                [Motion Blur]
                     Quadrant D    |    Quadrant C
                   (Fast & Narrow) |   (Slow & Narrow)
                                   |
                                   v
                         Small Aperture (f/32)
                         [Deep DOF / Less Light]
```

### Analysis of the Four Operating Quadrants:

1. **Quadrant A (Large Aperture + Fast Shutter):**
   - _Optical Effect:_ **Shallow Depth of Field (DOF)** with **Frozen Motion**.
   - _Visual Characteristic:_ Crisp foreground subject, heavily blurred background (bokeh), and high-speed motion captured without streak artifacts.
2. **Quadrant B (Large Aperture + Slow Shutter):**
   - _Optical Effect:_ Maximum light collection (**Bright** / Overexposed) with severe **Motion Blur** and **Shallow DOF**.
3. **Quadrant C (Small Aperture + Slow Shutter):**
   - _Optical Effect:_ **Deep Depth of Field (Deep DOF)** with **Blurred Motion**.
   - _Visual Characteristic:_ Landscape photography look; both near and far objects are in focus, but moving elements (e.g., water, moving cars) are smoothed into motion trails.
4. **Quadrant D (Small Aperture + Fast Shutter):**
   - _Optical Effect:_ Severely photon-starved (**Dark** / Underexposed) with **Frozen Motion** and **Deep DOF**.

## Part 1: Digitization — Spatial Sampling & Quantization (Slides 3–12)

Converting a continuous real-world scene $f(x, y)$ into a digital image $I[r, c]$ requires two distinct discretization steps:

```
Continuous Image f(x, y)
       │
       ▼  [Step 1: Spatial Sampling]  ---> Discretizes Coordinates (x, y) into (r, c)
Discrete Lattice Grid
       │
       ▼  [Step 2: Quantization]     ---> Discretizes Amplitude / Brightness into L levels
Digital Matrix I[r, c]
```

### 1. Spatial Sampling (Slides 4–6, 8–11)

- **Definition:** Discretization of the continuous spatial spatial domain into an $M \times N$ array of discrete grid elements (**pixels** or _pels_).
- **Sensor Resolution:** Determined by the physical sensor fabrication (e.g., Canon EOS 5DS: $8688 \times 5792 \approx 50.3\text{ Megapixels}$).
- **Image as a Matrix:** An image is a numerical 2D array:
  $$\mathbf{A}_{2 \times 2} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 64 \\ 128 & 255 \end{bmatrix}$$
  - A value of $0$ corresponds to pure black; $255$ represents maximum brightness (white).
- **Coordinate Systems (Slide 6):**
  - **0-indexed:** Origin $(0, 0)$ is at the top-left; rows $r \in [0, M-1]$, columns $c \in [0, N-1]$ (used in C/C++, Python, OpenCV).
  - **1-indexed:** Origin $(1, 1)$ is at the top-left; rows $r \in [1, M]$, columns $c \in [1, N]$ (used in MATLAB).
- **Sampling Defect — Block Artifacts (Slide 11):**
  - When an image is subsampled to an insufficient spatial resolution (e.g., downsampled from $1024 \times 1024$ to $32 \times 32$), the continuous contours break into visible square pixel blocks upon display.

---

### 2. Quantization (Slides 7, 12)

- **Definition:** Discretization of continuous light amplitude (radiance) into a finite set of $L$ integer intensity levels.
- **Hardware:** Performed by an **Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)**. In modern CMOS sensors, ADCs are placed on each column of the pixel array.
- **Bit Depth ($b$):**
  $$L = 2^b$$
  - _Monochromatic (Grayscale):_ 8 bits ($1\text{ Byte}$) $\implies 2^8 = 256$ intensity levels, $I \in [0, 255]$.
  - _True-Color RGB:_ 3 channels $\times$ 8 bits = 24 bits $\implies 2^{24} = 16,777,216$ colors.
- **Quantization Defect — False Contouring (Slide 12):**
  - When the number of quantization levels is severely restricted (e.g., dropping from 256 levels to 16, 8, 4, or 2 levels), smooth, continuous luminance gradients degrade into distinct, step-like bands known as **false contours**.

Dividing total capacity by the Byte rate per second:

## Part 2: The Sampling Theorem, Aliasing & Slit Animation (Slides 14–24, 44–46, 47–48)

### 1. The Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem (Slide 16)

To accurately reconstruct an analog signal of highest frequency $f_{\text{input}}$ without aliasing distortion, the sampling frequency $f_{\text{sample}}$ must be at least twice the maximum signal frequency:

$$f_{\text{sample}} \ge 2 f_{\text{input}} \iff \frac{f_{\text{sample}}}{2} \ge f_{\text{input}} \iff f_{\text{Nyquist}} \ge f_{\text{input}}$$

Where $f_{\text{Nyquist}} = \frac{f_{\text{sample}}}{2}$ is the **Nyquist frequency**.

#### Two Engineering Solutions to Prevent Aliasing:

1. **Increase $f_{\text{sample}}$:** Use a higher-density sensor array or higher temporal frame rate.
2. **Apply an Anti-Aliasing Filter:** Pass the input through an optical low-pass filter (OLPF) prior to sampling to eliminate frequencies above $f_{\text{Nyquist}}$.

### Fully Worked Exercise 2: The Sampling Theorem (Slide 17)

#### Problem Statement:

To represent the continuous signal $y$ without aliasing:
$$y = A \cos(Bx + C) + D$$
What is the minimum sampling frequency? Express it using pertinent variables from $A, B, C$, and $D$.

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

The standard mathematical form of a sinusoidal wave as a function of continuous coordinate $x$ is:
$$y = A \cos(2\pi f_{\text{input}} x + C) + D$$
Comparing this to the given expression $y = A \cos(Bx + C) + D$:

- The angular frequency is $\omega = B$.
- The cyclical frequency of the input signal is:
  $$2\pi f_{\text{input}} = B \implies f_{\text{input}} = \frac{B}{2\pi}$$
- According to the Nyquist criterion, the minimum sampling frequency $f_{\text{sample, min}}$ must satisfy:
  $$f_{\text{sample, min}} = 2 f_{\text{input}} = 2 \left(\frac{B}{2\pi}\right) = \frac{B}{\pi}$$

**Final Answer:**
$$f_{\text{sample, min}} = \frac{B}{\pi}$$
_(Variables $A$ [amplitude], $C$ [phase angle], and $D$ [DC bias offset] affect only signal magnitude and offset; they have no bearing on frequency or required sampling rate)._

---

### 2. Mathematical Prediction of Aliasing Frequencies (Slides 18, 20, 48)

When a signal exceeds the Nyquist limit ($f_{\text{input}} > f_{\text{sample}} / 2$), it folds back into the baseband $[0, f_{\text{sample}}/2]$ as an erroneous ghost frequency $f_{\text{aliasing}}$.

Slides 20 and 48 provide two analytical formulations to compute $f_{\text{aliasing}}$:

```
               Nyquist Limit (fs/2)
                      |
    0                 |                 fs               2fs
    +-----------------+-----------------+-----------------+-----> Frequency
    |    PASSBAND     |
    |  (No Aliasing)  |  Folds back     | Folds forward   |
    |                 |<--- (fs - r)    |<--- (r)         |
```

#### Method 1: The Modulo / Remainder Formulation (Slide 20, 48)

1. Calculate the remainder $r$ of the input frequency with respect to the sampling rate:
   $$r = \text{mod}(f_{\text{input}}, f_{\text{sample}})$$
2. The alias frequency is the minimum distance to the nearest sampling harmonic (the smaller of $r$ and $f_{\text{sample}} - r$):
   $$f_{\text{aliasing}} = \min(r, \, f_{\text{sample}} - r)$$

#### Method 2: The Nearest Multiple Formulation (Slide 48)

1. Find the integer multiple index $n$ nearest to the ratio:
   $$n = \text{round}\left(\frac{f_{\text{input}}}{f_{\text{sample}}}\right)$$
2. Calculate the folded absolute difference:
   $$f_{\text{aliasing}} = |f_{\text{input}} - n \cdot f_{\text{sample}}|$$

### Fully Worked Today's Quiz: Part 1 (Slides 47–48)

#### Problem Statement:

Given $f_{\text{input}} = 4700\text{ Hz}$ and $f_{\text{sample}} = 1000\text{ Hz}$.  
Find $f_{\text{aliasing}}$ using both **Solution (i)** and **Solution (ii)**.

---

#### Solution:

- **Solution (i) — Modulo / Remainder Method:**
  $$r = \text{mod}(f_{\text{input}}, f_{\text{sample}}) = \text{mod}(4700, 1000) = 700\text{ Hz}$$

**Result:** Both formulations yield $f_{\text{aliasing}} = \mathbf{300\text{ Hz}}$.

### 3. Slit Animation / Moiré Animation (Slides 24, 44–46)

While aliasing is typically an unwanted defect in computer vision, **Moiré animation** uses it systematically to generate motion illusions from static printed sheets:

1. **Prepare $n$ frames** representing a cyclic sequence of motion (e.g., $n = 3$ frames of a shifting square, or $n = 6$ frames of a galloping horse).
2. **Interleave the frames:** Construct a single composite image by interleaving vertical slices: column 1 from frame 1, column 2 from frame 2, ..., column $n$ from frame $n$, then repeating cyclically.
3. **Construct a sampling mask:** Create an opaque physical barrier sheet with transparent vertical slits having a repetition period of $n$ columns (width of transparent slit = 1 column, opaque barrier = $n - 1$ columns).
4. **Slide the mask:** As the barrier translates horizontally by $\Delta x = 1\text{ pixel}$, it alternately unmasks frame 1, frame 2, ..., frame $n$, producing smooth motion due to spatial subsampling.

## Part 3: Bit-Plane Decomposition & Run-Length Encoding (Slides 25–30, 47)

### 1. Bit-Plane Decomposition

Any 8-bit monochromatic image pixel can be represented as an 8-bit binary polynomial:
$$I(x,y) = b_7 \cdot 2^7 + b_6 \cdot 2^6 + b_5 \cdot 2^5 + b_4 \cdot 2^4 + b_3 \cdot 2^3 + b_2 \cdot 2^2 + b_1 \cdot 2^1 + b_0 \cdot 2^0$$
Decomposing an image creates **8 binary matrices** (Bit-planes 0 to 7):

- **Bit-plane 7 (MSB, weight 128):** Contains the primary structural layout, global contrast, and boundaries.
- **Bit-plane 0 (LSB, weight 1):** Appears as visually imperceptible random noise, making it suitable for steganography and fragile watermarking.

---

### Fully Worked Exercise 4: Bit-Plane Decomposition (Slide 26)

#### Problem Statement:

Perform bit-plane decomposition on the $2 \times 2$ grayscale image:
$$\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 57 \\ 215 & 120 \end{bmatrix}$$
What will happen to this image if the least significant bit (LSB) plane is discarded?

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

**Step 1: Convert all matrix entries to 8-bit unsigned binary ($b_7 b_6 b_5 b_4 b_3 b_2 b_1 b_0$).**

- **For 8:**
  $$8 = 0\cdot 128 + 0\cdot 64 + 0\cdot 32 + 0\cdot 16 + 1\cdot 8 + 0\cdot 4 + 0\cdot 2 + 0\cdot 1 \implies \mathbf{00001000_2}$$
- **For 57:**
  $$57 = 32 + 16 + 8 + 1 \implies \mathbf{00111001_2}$$
- **For 215:**
  $$215 = 128 + 64 + 16 + 4 + 2 + 1 \implies \mathbf{11010111_2}$$
  _(Slide 27 shows conversions via base-8 octal: $215_{10} = 327_8 \to 011\,010\,111_2$, and base-16 hex: $215_{10} = \text{D}7_{16} \to 1101\,0111_2$)_.
- **For 120:**
  $$120 = 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 \implies \mathbf{01111000_2}$$

---

**Step 2: Construct the 8 binary bit-plane matrices.**
Writing $\mathbf{I} = \sum_{k=0}^{7} \mathbf{P}_k \cdot 2^k$:

$$\mathbf{P}_7\ (\text{MSB} \times 128) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_6\ (\text{7th} \times 64) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_5\ (\text{6th} \times 32) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_4\ (\text{5th} \times 16) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_3\ (\text{4th} \times 8) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_2\ (\text{3rd} \times 4) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_1\ (\text{2nd} \times 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\mathbf{P}_0\ (\text{LSB} \times 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

---

**Step 3: Discard the LSB Plane ($\mathbf{P}_0$).**
Setting the LSB to 0 (or subtracting $\mathbf{P}_0 \times 1$):

- $8 \implies 0000100\mathbf{0}_2 = \mathbf{8}$
- $57 \implies 0011100\mathbf{0}_2 = 57 - 1 = \mathbf{56}$
- $215 \implies 1101011\mathbf{0}_2 = 215 - 1 = \mathbf{214}$
- $120 \implies 0111100\mathbf{0}_2 = \mathbf{120}$

The reconstructed image becomes:
$$\mathbf{I}_{\text{no LSB}} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 56 \\ 214 & 120 \end{bmatrix}$$
_Conclusion:_ Discarding the LSB changes pixel intensities by at most $1$ unit. This change is imperceptible to the human eye.

### 2. Run-Length Encoding (RLE) & Compression Metrics (Slides 28–30)

Run-Length Encoding (RLE) is a lossless data compression technique that replaces consecutive repeated symbols (runs) with a single counter value.

#### Example from Slide 28:

Given binary sequence:
$$\text{"}\underbrace{000000000}_{9\text{ zeros}}\underbrace{11111111}_{8\text{ ones}}\underbrace{00000}_{5\text{ zeros}}\underbrace{1111111111}_{10\text{ ones}}\underbrace{000}_{3\text{ zeros}}\text{"}$$
Alternative valid RLE formats:

1. Pair format: $(0, 9)(1, 8)(0, 5)(1, 10)(0, 3)$
2. Alternating run-count format (assuming sequence starts with 0): $(9)(8)(5)(10)(3)$
3. Transition coordinate format: $(10, 8)(23, 10)$, storing the 1-based start positions and run lengths of the ones.

---

#### Detailed Breakdown of Compression Ratio Example (Slide 30):

A binary image has dimensions:
$$M = 1024\text{ columns}, \quad N = 343\text{ rows}$$
After RLE, the entire image is reduced to **$12,166\text{ runs}$**.

- **Why are 11 bits necessary for each run?**
  - The maximum possible length of a continuous run across a row is the full image width: $1024\text{ pixels}$.
  - A 10-bit integer can only index $2^{10} = 1024$ states (values $0$ to $1023$).
  - To represent a maximum run of $1024$ without ambiguity (or values in range $[1, 1024]$), the required bit allocation is:
    $$b_{\text{run}} = \lceil \log_2(1024 + 1) \rceil = 11\text{ bits}$$
- **Original uncompressed bit count:**
  $$\text{Original Bits} = 1024 \times 343 \times 1\text{ bit} = 351,232\text{ bits}$$
- **Compressed code bit count:**
  $$\text{Code Bits} = 12,166\text{ runs} \times 11\text{ bits/run} = 133,826\text{ bits}$$
- **Compression Ratio ($C_R$):**
  $$C_R = \frac{\text{Original Bits}}{\text{Code Bits}} = \frac{351,232}{133,826} \approx \mathbf{2.62}$$
- **Relative Data Redundancy ($R_D$):**
  $$R_D = 1 - \frac{1}{C_R} = 1 - \frac{1}{2.6246} \approx 1 - 0.381 = \mathbf{0.62} \quad (62\%)$$
  _(This indicates that $62\%$ of the original data was redundant)._

## Part 4: Digital Watermarking (Slides 31–41)

Digital watermarking embeds verification data into an image for copyright management, tampering detection, and content authentication.

### 1. Visible Spatial-Domain Watermarking (Slides 33–34)

Blends a visible watermark pattern $w(x, y)$ over an image $f(x, y)$ using a linear transparency factor $\alpha \in [0, 1]$:
$$f_w(x, y) = (1 - \alpha) f(x, y) + \alpha \, w(x, y)$$

- When $\alpha = 0.3$, the watermark is semi-transparent ($70\%$ underlying image, $30\%$ watermark pattern).

---

### 2. Invisible LSB Spatial-Domain Watermarking (Slides 35–39)

Hides information in the least significant bits of the carrier image.

#### The 2-LSB Insertion Formula (Slide 35):

$$f_w = 2^2 \times \left\lfloor \frac{f}{2^2} \right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac{w}{2^6} \right\rfloor$$
This operation works as follows:

- Dividing $f$ by $2^2 = 4$, taking the floor, and multiplying by $4$ clears (zeros out) the two least significant bits of $f$:
  $$2^2 \times \left\lfloor \frac{f}{2^2} \right\rfloor \equiv f\ \&\ 11111100_2 \quad (\text{Bitwise AND with } 252)$$
- Dividing $w$ by $2^6 = 64$ and taking the floor isolates its two most significant bits ($b_7, b_6$) and shifts them to positions $b_1, b_0$:
  $$\left\lfloor \frac{w}{2^6} \right\rfloor \equiv w \gg 6$$
- The sum places the two MSBs of $w$ directly into the two vacated LSB positions of $f$.

#### Extraction Formula:

To retrieve the embedded watermark $\hat{w}$:
$$\hat{w} = (f_w\ \&\ 00000011_2) \times 64$$

#### Fragility and Compression Vulnerability (Slide 38):

Spatial LSB watermarks are **fragile**. When an image undergoes JPEG lossy compression, the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) combined with high-frequency quantization discards subtle spatial variations. The reconstructed LSBs are corrupted (RMS error $\approx 2.1\text{ bits}$), rendering the extracted watermark illegible.

### Fully Worked Exercise 5: Watermarking in 2 LSBs (Slides 36–37)

#### Problem Statement:

Using the equation $f_w = 2^2 \times \lfloor f / 2^2 \rfloor + \lfloor w / 2^6 \rfloor$, embed watermark $w$ into carrier image $f$:
$$f = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 57 \\ 215 & 120 \end{bmatrix}, \quad w = \begin{bmatrix} 206 & 173 \\ 68 & 39 \end{bmatrix}$$

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

**Step 1: Compute the modified carrier terms $4 \times \lfloor f / 4 \rfloor$ (zeroing the 2 LSBs).**

- $f(1,1) = 8$:  
  $\lfloor 8 / 4 \rfloor = 2 \implies 2 \times 4 = \mathbf{8}$  
  _(Binary: $000010\mathbf{00}_2$)_
- $f(1,2) = 57$:  
  $\lfloor 57 / 4 \rfloor = 14 \implies 14 \times 4 = \mathbf{56}$  
  _(Binary: $001110\mathbf{00}_2$)_
- $f(2,1) = 215$:  
  $\lfloor 215 / 4 \rfloor = 53 \implies 53 \times 4 = \mathbf{212}$  
  _(Binary: $110101\mathbf{00}_2$)_
- $f(2,2) = 120$:  
  $\lfloor 120 / 4 \rfloor = 30 \implies 30 \times 4 = \mathbf{120}$  
  _(Binary: $011110\mathbf{00}_2$)_

---

**Step 2: Compute the watermark terms $\lfloor w / 64 \rfloor$ (extracting the 2 MSBs).**

- $w(1,1) = 206$:  
  $206 = \mathbf{11}001110_2 \implies \lfloor 206 / 64 \rfloor = \mathbf{3}$  
  _(Binary: $\mathbf{11}_2$)_
- $w(1,2) = 173$:  
  $173 = \mathbf{10}101101_2 \implies \lfloor 173 / 64 \rfloor = \mathbf{2}$  
  _(Binary: $\mathbf{10}_2$)_
- $w(2,1) = 68$:  
  $68 = \mathbf{01}000100_2 \implies \lfloor 68 / 64 \rfloor = \mathbf{1}$  
  _(Binary: $\mathbf{01}_2$)_
- $w(2,2) = 39$:  
  $39 = \mathbf{00}100111_2 \implies \lfloor 39 / 64 \rfloor = \mathbf{0}$  
  _(Binary: $\mathbf{00}_2$)_

---

**Step 3: Sum the two components to form $f_w$.**

- Top-Left: $8 + 3 = \mathbf{11}$
- Top-Right: $56 + 2 = \mathbf{58}$
- Bottom-Left: $212 + 1 = \mathbf{213}$
- Bottom-Right: $120 + 0 = \mathbf{120}$

$$f_w = \begin{bmatrix} 11 & 58 \\ 213 & 120 \end{bmatrix}$$
_(This matches the professor's handwritten result on Slide 37)._

### Fully Worked Exercise 6: Spatial-Domain Watermarking (Slide 40)

#### Problem Statement:

An alphabet letter is embedded into the LSB plane of the following $8 \times 8$ image. Retrieve and identify the letter.

$$
\mathbf{I}_{8 \times 8} = \begin{bmatrix}
4 & 8 & 12 & 16 & 2 & 0 & 10 & 4 \\
12 & 5 & 17 & 15 & 15 & 9 & 7 & 6 \\
14 & 13 & 9 & 7 & 17 & 7 & 7 & 6 \\
6 & 8 & 4 & 9 & 13 & 0 & 12 & 8 \\
10 & 20 & 6 & 11 & 11 & 2 & 12 & 0 \\
12 & 2 & 14 & 5 & 7 & 4 & 10 & 10 \\
8 & 4 & 8 & 9 & 17 & 14 & 8 & 12 \\
0 & 4 & 2 & 12 & 6 & 6 & 10 & 8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

---

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

To retrieve the LSB plane, compute $\text{LSB}(x, y) = I(x, y) \pmod 2$:

- If the pixel value is **even**, $\text{LSB} = \mathbf{0}$.
- If the pixel value is **odd**, $\text{LSB} = \mathbf{1}$.

**Row-by-Row Parity Check:**

- **Row 1:** $[4, 8, 12, 16, 2, 0, 10, 4] \implies \text{all even} \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0}]$
- **Row 2:** $[12, 5, 17, 15, 15, 9, 7, 6] \implies [\mathbf{0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0}]$
- **Row 3:** $[14, 13, 9, 7, 17, 7, 7, 6] \implies [\mathbf{0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0}]$
- **Row 4:** $[6, 8, 4, 9, 13, 0, 12, 8] \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0}]$
- **Row 5:** $[10, 20, 6, 11, 11, 2, 12, 0] \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0}]$
- **Row 6:** $[12, 2, 14, 5, 7, 4, 10, 10] \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0}]$
- **Row 7:** $[8, 4, 8, 9, 17, 14, 8, 12] \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0}]$
- **Row 8:** $[0, 4, 2, 12, 6, 6, 10, 8] \implies \text{all even} \implies [\mathbf{0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0}]$
