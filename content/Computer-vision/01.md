## 1. Camera Architecture & Comparison

Digital cameras are categorized into three main architectures based on their internal optical paths, sensor integration, and viewfinder mechanisms:

```
A: Compact Digital Camera           B: Digital Single-Lens Reflex (DSLR)
   +-----------+                       +-------Pentaprism (7)------+
   |     Lens  |                       |             \     OVF (4) |
   | [1] ====> |======> Sensor [2]     |              \   /        |
   +-----------+           |           |   Lens        [5] Mirror  |
                           v           |    [1] =====>  /          |
                     LCD Screen [3]    |               /==> Sensor |
                                       +--------------------[2]----+
```

### Architectural Comparison

| Feature                | Compact Camera                                                                                   | DSLR Camera                                                                                 | Mirrorless Camera                                                                        |
| :--------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Optical Path**       | Direct to sensor. No internal mirror or prism.                                                   | Lens $\to$ Reflex Mirror (45°) $\to$ Pentaprism $\to$ OVF. Mirror flips up during exposure. | Direct to sensor. No reflex mirror, no pentaprism.                                       |
| **Viewfinder**         | Back LCD monitor (**Live View** only).                                                           | **Optical Viewfinder (OVF)** via pentaprism.                                                | **Electronic Viewfinder (EVF)** or back LCD monitor.                                     |
| **Latency / Time Lag** | **High latency** (Sensor readout $\to$ image processor $\to$ LCD refresh). Poor for fast action. | **Zero latency** (Photons travel at speed of light directly through glass/mirrors to eye).  | **Low-to-medium latency** (High-speed sensor readout feeds miniature EVF micro-display). |
| **Lens Mount**         | **Fixed, non-interchangeable** integrated zoom lens.                                             | **Interchangeable** lenses with diverse focal lengths ($f$).                                | **Interchangeable** lenses (shorter flange focal distance).                              |
| **Sensor Size**        | Small (e.g., $1/2.3''$, $7.5 \times 5.0\text{ mm}$).                                             | Large (APS-C $22.2 \times 14.8\text{ mm}$, Full Frame $36 \times 24\text{ mm}$).            | Large (Micro 4/3 $17.3 \times 13\text{ mm}$, APS-C, Full Frame).                         |
| **Power Consumption**  | High (sensor and screen must be powered continuously).                                           | Low during framing (passive optical path; zero sensor power until shutter release).         | Moderate/High (EVF/LCD and sensor are continuously active).                              |

### Engineering Takeaways: EVF vs. OVF vs. LCD

1. **DSLR OVF:** Superior for high-speed sports/action tracking because of true real-time response (zero display lag) and zero power drain while framing.
2. **Mirrorless EVF:** Shows exposure preview, real-time histograms, and focus peaking directly inside the eyepiece. In bright sunlight, an EVF avoids screen glare that makes external LCDs unreadable. Holding the camera against your face also provides a third point of physical stabilization compared to holding an LCD at arm's length.

## 2. Image Sensors: CCD vs. CMOS

Both sensor types use the photoelectric effect: silicon photodiodes (PD) convert incident photons into bound electron-hole pairs (charge). Their difference lies in **how that charge is transported, converted to voltage, and amplified**.

```
CCD Architecture (Sequential Charge Transfer):
   [PD] [PD] [PD]  ==> Charges shifted down
    |    |    |        Vertical Shift Registers (V-SR)
    v    v    v
   [============== Horizontal Shift Register (H-SR) ===============]
                                                          |
                                           Single Output Amplifier + ADC

CMOS Architecture (Active Pixel Sensor - Parallel Readout):
   [PD + Amp] --- [PD + Amp] --- [PD + Amp]  ==> Row Select Line
        |              |              |
   Column Line    Column Line    Column Line
        v              v              v
   [Col ADC]      [Col ADC]      [Col ADC]   ==> Parallel Conversion
```

### Deep Architectural Comparison

| Characteristic                   | CCD (Charge-Coupled Device)                                                                     | CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide Semiconductor)                                                         |
| :------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Charge-to-Voltage Conversion** | Outside the active pixel array at a **single common output amplifier**.                         | **Inside every individual pixel** (each pixel contains its own photodiode, reset gate, and amplifier). |
| **Readout Mode**                 | **Bucket-brigade charge shifting** across vertical and horizontal registers.                    | Direct row/column addressing (X-Y coordinate addressable). Read out **row-by-row**.                    |
| **Readout Speed**                | Slower (all charges must pass through the single output node).                                  | Extremely fast (massively parallel column-level ADCs).                                                 |
| **Image Uniformity**             | **Very High** (All charges are amplified by the exact same physical circuit).                   | **Lower** (Microscopic fabrication mismatches between pixel amplifiers create Fixed-Pattern Noise).    |
| **Power Dissipation**            | **High** (Capacitive clocking lines drive shift registers across the entire chip continuously). | **Low** (Only the circuits on the actively addressed row consume switching power).                     |
| **Shutter Type**                 | Naturally supports **Global Shutter**.                                                          | Typically uses **Rolling Shutter** (unless specialized electronic global shutter circuitry is added).  |

### Characteristic Sensor Artifacts (Exam High-Yield)

#### 1. CCD Artifacts: Charge Smearing and Blooming

- **Blooming:** When high-intensity light (e.g., direct sun, headlights) hits a photodiode, the generated electron count exceeds the well capacity (saturation charge limit). Excess electrons spill over into adjacent horizontal and vertical photodiode wells, causing a large white circular halo.
- **Charge Smearing:** Occurs during the vertical charge transfer in a CCD. If intense light continues to strike the sensor while charges are being shifted down the vertical shift registers (V-SR), unwanted electrons leak into the packets moving underneath. This creates a distinct, continuous **bright vertical line** passing through the highlight across the entire image.

```
Blooming:                           Charge Smearing (CCD):
     . . . .                             |   |
   . . ( O ) . .  <-- Well overflow      | O |  <-- Light leaks into V-SR
     . . . .          into neighbors     |   |      during vertical transfer
                                         |   |
```

#### 2. CMOS Artifact: The Rolling Shutter Effect

Because CMOS sensors read out data line-by-line, Row $0$ is exposed at time $t_0$, while Row $M-1$ is exposed at time $t_0 + \Delta t$.

- If an object moves at high angular or linear velocity across the frame during this interval $\Delta t$, the top of the object is recorded at an earlier position than the bottom.
- **Visual Distortions:**
  - _Rotating objects (propellers, fan blades):_ Blades curve into spiral shapes.
  - _Translating objects (fast train, golf club):_ Vertical structures shear horizontally (skew).
  - _Horizontal camera panning (wobble/jello effect):_ Vertical telephone poles lean to the side.

### Sensor Size vs. Pixel Count

Market specifications emphasize **pixel resolution** (e.g., 48 MP), but computer vision performance depends fundamentally on **physical sensor area** ($A_{\text{sensor}}$).

$$\text{Pixel Pitch Area} \approx \frac{A_{\text{sensor}}}{\text{Total Pixel Count}}$$

```
Sensor Dimensions:
+-------------------------------------------------------+
| Full Frame (36 x 24 mm)                               |
|   +------------------------------------+              |
|   | APS-C (~23.6 x 15.6 mm)            |              |
|   |   +--------------------------+     |              |
|   |   | Micro 4/3 (17.3 x 13 mm) |     |              |
|   |   |   +----------------+     |     |              |
|   |   |   | 1" (12.8x9.6mm)|     |     |              |
|   |   |   |   +---------+  |     |     |              |
|   |   |   |   | 1/2.3"  |  |     |     |              |
+---+---+---+---+---------+--+-----+-----+--------------+
```

- **Why Larger Sensors Outperform Smaller Sensors with Equal Pixel Counts:**
  1.  **Low-Light Sensitivity & SNR:** Larger pixels collect a larger absolute number of photons per exposure:
      $$\text{SNR} = \frac{N_{\text{signal}}}{\sqrt{N_{\text{signal}} + \sigma_{\text{read}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{thermal}}^2}} \approx \sqrt{N_{\text{signal}}}$$
      More photons collected per pixel improves the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (less visible grain).
  2.  **Dynamic Range:** Larger photodiodes have higher full-well capacity, allowing them to capture bright highlights without clipping while still resolving shadow details above the noise floor.
  3.  **Depth of Field Control:** Larger sensors require longer focal lengths to maintain the same field of view, producing shallower depth of field (desirable for object isolation/segmentation).

## 3. Thin Lens Optics (Rigorous Derivations & Examples)

### The Three Fundamental Paraxial Rays

```
             Ray 1: Parallel to axis ----> Through focus F on far side
             Ray 2: Through center -------------------------> Undeviated
             Ray 3: Through focus F on near side ---> Emerges parallel
                      Lens Plane
                          |
     Object       \       |       /
      (h_o)        \      |      /
      |--->         \     |     /
   ---|------------- \ ---|--- / ------------------------- Axis
      |<----- p ----->|   |   |<----- q ----->|
                          |                   v Real Image (h_i)
```

1.  **Paraxial Ray (Ray 1):** Travels parallel to the optical axis, refracts through the lens, and passes through the real focal point $F$ on the opposite side.
2.  **Center Ray (Ray 2):** Passes directly through the optical center of the lens. Because the glass faces at the center are parallel and lens thickness $D \approx 0$, the ray passes through **without displacement or deviation**.
3.  **Focal Ray (Ray 3):** Passes through the near focal point $F$, strikes the lens, and emerges parallel to the optical axis on the opposite side.

---

### Exercise 1: Geometric Derivation of the Thin Lens Equation (Slides 30–31)

#### 1. Derive the Thin Lens Equation using Similar Triangles

Consider an object of height $h_o$ placed at distance $p$ in front of a converging lens of focal length $f$. A real, inverted image of height $h_i$ forms at distance $q$ behind the lens.

```
       A
       |\
       | \  Center Ray
   h_o |  \
       |   \
   ----B----+------------------+---------- Optical Axis
            | \                |
            |  \               | h_i
            |   \              |
                 \             v
                  +------------C
```

**Step 1: Triangle pair from the optical center ray**  
The triangle formed by the object and center $\triangle ABO$ is similar to the triangle formed by the image and center $\triangle C I O$:
$$\frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{q}{p} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

**Step 2: Triangle pair from the paraxial ray refracted through focus $F$**  
The paraxial ray leaves the top of the object parallel to the axis, hits the lens at height $h_o$, and passes through the far focal point $F$ to reach the image plane at distance $q$. The distance from $F$ to the image plane is $(q - f)$.  
By similar triangles across focal point $F$:
$$\frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{q - f}{f} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

**Step 3: Equate the two geometric expressions**
$$\frac{q}{p} = \frac{q - f}{f}$$
$$\frac{q}{p} = \frac{q}{f} - 1$$

Divide the entire equation by $q$ (assuming $q \neq 0$):
$$\frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{q}$$

Rearranging gives the **Gaussian Thin Lens Equation**:
$$\mathbf{\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f}}$$

---

#### 2. Find the condition for Magnification Rate $r = 1$ ($h_i = h_o$)

Magnification rate is defined as:
$$r = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{q}{p}$$
For $r = 1$:
$$\frac{q}{p} = 1 \implies p = q$$
Substitute $q = p$ into the thin lens equation:
$$\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{f} \implies \frac{2}{p} = \frac{1}{f} \implies \mathbf{p = 2f}$$
And since $q = p$:
$$\mathbf{q = 2f}$$
**Condition:** The object must be placed at exactly twice the focal length ($2f$) in front of the lens. The real image forms at $2f$ behind the lens, inverted and at a $1:1$ scale ratio.

### Exercise 2: Convex Thin Lens — Magnifying Glass (Slide 33)

**Problem:**  
A converging lens has focal length $f = +20\text{ cm}$. An object is placed inside the focal point at distance $p = 12\text{ cm}$.

1. Where is the image formed?
2. What is the magnification rate $r$?

**Solution:**  
Using the thin lens formula:
$$\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f}$$
$$\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{20}$$
$$\frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{12}$$
Find a common denominator ($60$):
$$\frac{1}{q} = \frac{3 - 5}{60} = -\frac{2}{60} = -\frac{1}{30}$$
$$\mathbf{q = -30\text{ cm}}$$

**Interpretation:** The negative sign indicates a **virtual, upright image** located $30\text{ cm}$ in front of the lens (on the same side as the object).

Magnification rate:
$$r = \frac{|q|}{p} = \frac{30}{12} = \mathbf{2.5}$$
The virtual image is upright and magnified by a factor of $2.5\times$.

### Exercise 3: Concave Thin Lens (Slide 35)

**Problem:**  
A diverging lens has focal length $f = -20\text{ cm}$. An object is placed at distance $p = 30\text{ cm}$.

1. Where is the image formed?
2. What is the magnification rate $r$?

**Solution:**  
For a diverging (concave) lens, $f$ is negative ($f = -20\text{ cm}$):
$$\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f}$$
$$\frac{1}{30} + \frac{1}{q} = -\frac{1}{20}$$
$$\frac{1}{q} = -\frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{30}$$
Common denominator ($60$):
$$\frac{1}{q} = \frac{-3 - 2}{60} = -\frac{5}{60} = -\frac{1}{12}$$
$$\mathbf{q = -12\text{ cm}}$$

Magnification rate:
$$r = \frac{|q|}{p} = \frac{12}{30} = \mathbf{0.4}$$

**Interpretation:** Diverging lenses always produce a **virtual, upright, diminished** image ($r < 1$) located between the lens and the object.

### Exercise 4: Two-Lens System (Slides 36–37)

**Problem:**  
A compound optical system consists of two lenses separated by a distance $d = 40\text{ mm}$:

- Lens 1 ($L_1$): Converging lens, focal length $f_1 = +20\text{ mm}$.
- Lens 2 ($L_2$): Diverging lens, focal length $f_2 = -35\text{ mm}$.
- An object is placed at $p_1 = 30\text{ mm}$ in front of $L_1$.

1. Where does the final image form relative to Lens 2 ($L_2$)?
2. What is the total system magnification $r$?

```
Ray Trace Layout:
     Object           Lens 1                 Lens 2
       |              (f1=20)                (f2=-35)
   ----+----------------|-----------------------)-------------------- Axis
       |<--- 30mm ----->|                       |
                        |<------- 40mm -------->|
                        |<------------- q1 = 60mm ------------->| (Image 1)
                                                |<-- p2=-20mm ->|
                                                |<---- q2 = 46.67mm ---->| (Final)
```

#### Step-by-Step Solution:

**Stage 1: Image formation through Lens 1 ($L_1$)**
$$\frac{1}{p_1} + \frac{1}{q_1} = \frac{1}{f_1}$$
$$\frac{1}{30} + \frac{1}{q_1} = \frac{1}{20} \implies \frac{1}{q_1} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{30} = \frac{3 - 2}{60} = \frac{1}{60}$$
$$q_1 = +60\text{ mm}$$
Magnification of $L_1$:
$$r_1 = \frac{q_1}{p_1} = \frac{60}{30} = 2$$
The intermediate image $I_1$ forms **$60\text{ mm}$ behind $L_1$**, inverted and scaled $2\times$.

---

**Stage 2: Image formation through Lens 2 ($L_2$)**  
The two lenses are separated by $d = 40\text{ mm}$.  
Because the rays from $L_1$ converge toward a point $60\text{ mm}$ behind $L_1$, they intercept $L_2$ before reaching that focus.  
$I_1$ is located behind $L_2$ by:
$$60\text{ mm} - 40\text{ mm} = 20\text{ mm} \text{ behind } L_2$$

Therefore, $I_1$ acts as a **Virtual Object** for $L_2$:
$$p_2 = -20\text{ mm}$$

Applying the thin lens formula to $L_2$ with $f_2 = -35\text{ mm}$:
$$\frac{1}{p_2} + \frac{1}{q_2} = \frac{1}{f_2}$$
$$\frac{1}{-20} + \frac{1}{q_2} = \frac{1}{-35}$$
$$\frac{1}{q_2} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{35}$$
Find a common denominator ($\text{LCM}(20, 35) = 140$):
$$\frac{1}{q_2} = \frac{7 - 4}{140} = \frac{3}{140}$$
$$\mathbf{q_2 = \frac{140}{3} \approx +46.67\text{ mm}}$$

Because $q_2 > 0$, the final image is **real** and forms **$46.67\text{ mm}$ behind Lens 2**.

---

**Stage 3: Total magnification**
Magnification through $L_2$:
$$r_2 = \frac{q_2}{|p_2|} = \frac{140 / 3}{20} = \frac{140}{60} = \frac{7}{3}$$

Total system magnification:
$$\mathbf{r = r_1 \times r_2 = 2 \times \frac{7}{3} = \frac{14}{3} \approx 4.67}$$

The final image is real, inverted (relative to the original object), and magnified **$4.67\times$**.

## 4. Field of View & Perspective Geometry

```
                    Angle of View (alpha)
                        /|
                       / |
       d (Aperture)   /  |
        +---+        /   |
        |   |       /    |
   =====+   +======*     | Focal Plane
        |   |    f  \    |
        +---+        \   |
                      \  |
                       \|
```

### Angle of View (Field of View - FOV) (Slide 38)

Let $d$ be the effective dimension (or aperture limit) and $f$ be the lens focal length. The angle subtended at the focus is:

$$\mathbf{\alpha = 2 \arctan\left(\frac{d}{2f}\right)}$$

- **Focal Length Trade-Off:**
  - **Short $f$ (Wide-Angle, e.g., $18\text{ mm}$):** Yields large $\alpha$. Captures wide scenes, but introduces perspective exaggeration.
  - **Long $f$ (Telephoto, e.g., $200\text{ mm} - 300\text{ mm}$):** Yields small $\alpha$. Magnifies distant objects, but narrows the visible scene.

### Perspective vs. Distance & The Compression Effect (Slides 39–41)

- **Perspective is determined solely by the camera-to-subject distance**, not by focal length alone.
- **Compression Effect:** When shooting a subject from far away with a long telephoto lens ($200\text{ mm}$):
  - The subject appears close, but background elements (such as distant mountains) appear disproportionately large and compressed against the subject.
  - With a wide-angle lens ($24\text{ mm}$), moving close to keep the foreground object the same size causes background elements to appear distant and small.

## 5. The Exposure Triangle: Shutter Speed, Aperture, and ISO

Exposure determines the total energy density incident on the sensor:

$$\text{Exposure} \propto \frac{\Delta t \cdot \text{Gain}}{N^2}$$

```
                                EXPOSURE
                     +-------------+-------------+
                     |                           |
             Shutter Speed (t)             Aperture (f-number N)
             [Motion Blur]                 [Depth of Field]
                     \                           /
                      \                         /
                       +---- ISO Sensitivity ---+
                             [Sensor Noise]
```

### 1. Shutter Speed ($\Delta t$) (Slides 44–45)

- Controls the duration over which the sensor integrates light.
- **Fast Shutter ($1/1600\text{ s}$):** Freezes high-speed movement (e.g., sports, flying debris). Requires strong illumination or a wide aperture.
- **Slow Shutter ($5\text{ s}$):** Smooths continuous movement (e.g., flowing water, light trails). Introduces motion blur for moving targets.

### 2. Aperture & F-Number ($N$) (Slides 46, 53–55)

The **F-number** (or F-stop) is the ratio of focal length $f$ to effective aperture entrance pupil diameter $d$:

$$\mathbf{\text{F-number} = \frac{f}{d} \implies d = \frac{f}{\text{F-number}}}$$

_Notation Note (Slide 55):_ The notation "$f/2.8$" means the aperture diameter is $d = \frac{f}{2.8}$. The slash represents a ratio of $f$ to $N$, not mathematical division.

#### Standard Geometric F-Stop Progression:

$$[\mathbf{1.4, \; 2, \; 2.8, \; 4, \; 5.6, \; 8, \; 11, \; 16, \; 22}]$$
Each consecutive step scales $N$ up by a factor of $\mathbf{\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414}$:
$$2.8 \times \sqrt{2} \approx 4, \quad 4 \times \sqrt{2} \approx 5.6, \quad 5.6 \times \sqrt{2} \approx 8, \dots$$

### Exercise 6: F-Numbers and Aperture Area (Slide 56)

**Question:** Show the relationship between the geometric sequence $[2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16, 22]$ and the aperture area.

#### Derivation:

The light entering the camera is proportional to the circular aperture area $A$:
$$A = \pi \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$$
Since $d = \frac{f}{N}$:
$$A = \frac{\pi f^2}{4 N^2} \propto \mathbf{\frac{1}{N^2}}$$

When transitioning to the next full stop ($N_{k+1} = \sqrt{2} \cdot N_k$):
$$\frac{A_{k+1}}{A_k} = \frac{\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2} N_k)^2}}{\frac{1}{N_k^2}} = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Conclusion:** Each time the F-number increases by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, the aperture opening diameter scales down by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, and the physical light-collecting area is **reduced by half ($50\%$)**.

---

### 3. ISO Sensitivity (Gain) (Slides 48–50)

- The sensor photodiode has a fixed physical quantum efficiency. **ISO is analog/digital amplification** applied to the readout signal.
- **Positive Effect:** Doubling ISO ($100 \to 200$) allows doubling the shutter speed ($1/50\text{ s} \to 1/100\text{ s}$) under the same lighting, helping freeze action without underexposing.
- **Negative Effect:** The amplifier gain boosts both the signal and the underlying noise floor ($\sigma_{\text{thermal}}, \sigma_{\text{read}}$). High ISO values (e.g., ISO 3200+) introduce strong visible noise, lowering image quality.

## 6. Depth of Field (DOF) & The Circle of Confusion

### Geometric Focus & Depth of Field (Slides 51–53)

From the thin lens equation, only objects located at an exact physical distance $p$ focus to an infinitesimal point on a sensor placed at distance $q$:
$$p = \frac{f q}{q - f}$$
Points in front of or behind $p$ form a cone of light that projects onto the sensor as a circular blur:

```
                            Lens                     Sensor Plane
   Object Near               |                           |
   -----(o)------------------|----\                      |
                             |     \                     |
   Object Focused            |      \-----\              |
   ------(O)-----------------|-------------*-------------|-- (Sharp point focus)
                             |            /              |
   Object Far                |      /----/               |
   -------(o)----------------|-----/          \          |
                             |                 \         |
                             |                  \========|== Blur Circle
                                                         |<-> Circle of Confusion (CoC)
```

- **Circle of Confusion (CoC):** The largest blur circle diameter that remains indistinguishable from a sharp point to a human observer (or relative to the sensor pixel size).
- **Depth of Field (DOF):** The acceptable zone of sharpness in object space over which the resulting blur circle is smaller than the CoC limit.

### Exercise 7: DOF and F-Numbers Case Study (Slides 57–58)

The slide presents three images of a slanted microchip taken with varying exposure settings:

1. $f/5$ at $t = 0.2\text{ s}$
2. $f/14$ at $t = 1.6\text{ s}$
3. $f/32$ at $t = 8.0\text{ s}$

```
Microchip Image at f/5, 0.2s:       Microchip Image at f/32, 8.0s:
+------------------------+           +------------------------+
| Blur                   |           | Pin-sharp              |
| Sharp text [ 17301Q1 ] |           | Pin-sharp [ 17301Q1 ]  |
| Blur                   |           | Pin-sharp              |
+------------------------+           +------------------------+
(Narrow/Shallow DOF)                 (Deep/Wide DOF)
```

#### 1. Explain the relationship between F-numbers and the resulting DOFs:

- At **$f/5$ (Small F-number $\to$ Large Aperture)**: The light rays converge at a steep angle. The blur cone expands rapidly away from the plane of focus, making the Depth of Field **narrow/shallow**. Only the center text row on the slanted chip is sharp; the top and bottom rows are heavily blurred.
- At **$f/32$ (Large F-number $\to$ Small Aperture)**: The aperture narrows the cone of light to a narrow beam. The blur circle grows very slowly with distance, producing a **deep/wide DOF**. The entire slanted microchip stays in sharp focus.

#### 2. Explain why the shutter speed is varied from $0.2\text{ s} \to 1.6\text{ s} \to 8.0\text{ s}$:

To maintain constant exposure as the aperture diameter is narrowed, the exposure time must increase proportionally to balance the loss of light:

1. **Step 1 ($f/5 \to f/14$):**
   $$\text{Aperture Area Ratio} = \left(\frac{5}{14}\right)^2 \approx \frac{25}{196} \approx \frac{1}{7.84} \approx \frac{1}{8}$$
   The aperture transmits approximately $\frac{1}{8}$ as much light. To keep exposure constant, shutter speed must increase by $8\times$:
   $$t_2 = 0.2\text{ s} \times 8 = \mathbf{1.6\text{ s}}$$

2. **Step 2 ($f/14 \to f/32$):**
   $$\text{Aperture Area Ratio} = \left(\frac{14}{32}\right)^2 \approx \left(\frac{7}{16}\right)^2 = \frac{49}{256} \approx \frac{1}{5.2} \approx \frac{1}{5}$$
   The aperture transmits approximately $\frac{1}{5}$ as much light. Shutter speed must increase by $5\times$:
   $$t_3 = 1.6\text{ s} \times 5 = \mathbf{8.0\text{ s}}$$

**Conclusion:** The shutter speed is varied across $0.2\text{ s} \to 1.6\text{ s} \to 8.0\text{ s}$ to maintain uniform overall image exposure across all shots.

### Exercise 8: Practical Engineering & Photography Scenarios (Slides 61–62)

```
               \

        (o)      \    (O)

```

#### 1. When taking a photo in the dark, should the F-number be small or large?

- **Answer:** **Small** (e.g., $f/1.4$ or $f/2.0$).
- _Reason:_ A small F-number corresponds to a large physical aperture opening ($d = f/N$), allowing the maximum number of photons to reach the sensor in low light.

#### 2. When you want to gain a wide depth of field (DOF), should the F-number be small or large?

- **Answer:** **Large** (e.g., $f/11$, $f/16$, or $f/22$).
- _Reason:_ A large F-number constricts the light cone, keeping the blur circle within the Circle of Confusion across a wider range of object distances.

#### 3. When you want to take a portrait shot with a smooth, blurred background (bokeh), should the F-number be small or large?

- **Answer:** **Small** (e.g., $f/1.8$ or $f/2.8$).
- _Reason:_ A small F-number creates a shallow depth of field. Only the subject's face remains sharp, while foreground and background elements quickly blur into an out-of-focus background.

#### 4. When you take an oblique (angled) portrait shot, which eye should be the main target to focus on?

- **Answer:** The **near eye** (the eye physically closest to the camera lens).
- _Reason:_ With shallow portrait depth of field, only one eye can be sharply in focus. In human portrait evaluation, a sharp near eye looks natural and intentional, whereas an out-of-focus near eye paired with a sharp far eye creates an unsettling blur in the foreground.

## 7. Quick Exam Review Summary

```
                      CORE EQUATIONS CHEAT SHEET
Thin Lens Equation:          1/p + 1/q = 1/f   (f > 0: Convex, f < 0: Concave)
Magnification Rate:          r = |q| / p
F-Number:                    N = f / d
Aperture Area Scaling:       Area ~ 1 / N^2
Angle of View:               alpha = 2 * arctan( d / (2f) )
Exposure Balance:            Exposure ~ (t * ISO) / N^2
```

- **CCD:** Global shutter, single output amplifier, high image uniformity, high power dissipation. Vulnerable to **smearing** (vertical lines) and **blooming** (saturation bleed).
- **CMOS:** Rolling shutter, in-pixel amplifiers, parallel ADC, low power consumption, higher fixed-pattern noise. Vulnerable to **rolling shutter distortion** on fast-moving targets.
- **Convex Lens ($f > 0$):**
  - $p > 2f \implies$ real, inverted, diminished image ($f < q < 2f$).
  - $p = 2f \implies$ real, inverted image with $1:1$ scale ($q = 2f$).
  - $f < p < 2f \implies$ real, inverted, magnified image ($q > 2f$).
  - $p < f \implies$ virtual, upright, magnified image on the object side ($q < 0$).
- **Concave Lens ($f < 0$):**
  - Always yields a **virtual, upright, diminished** image ($q < 0$, $r < 1$).
- **DOF Trade-Off:**
  - Small F-number $\to$ large aperture $\to$ shallow DOF, higher light intake.
  - Large F-number $\to$ small aperture $\to$ deep DOF, lower light intake (requires longer shutter speed or higher ISO).

Here is a complete breakdown of how increasing (larger) or decreasing (lower) the value of each parameter affects the final image.

---

### 1. Shutter Speed (Exposure Time)

_Note on terminology: In photography, "larger value" usually refers to a **longer exposure time** (e.g., 2 seconds vs. 1/1000s)._

#### **Larger Value / Longer Duration (e.g., 1/2s, 2s, 10s)**

- **Exposure:** **Brighter image.** The shutter stays open longer, allowing more light to hit the sensor.
- **Motion Blur:** Moving subjects become blurred or streaked (intentional effects include silky waterfalls, car light trails, and star trails).
- **Camera Shake:** Handheld shooting becomes prone to blur caused by hand tremors (often requires a tripod or image stabilization at speeds slower than ~1/60s).

#### **Lower Value / Shorter Duration (e.g., 1/500s, 1/2000s, 1/8000s)**

- **Exposure:** **Darker image.** The shutter opens and closes very quickly, limiting the light entering the camera.
- **Motion Freeze:** Fast-moving action is frozen crisply (e.g., water droplets, sports players, flying birds).
- **Sharpness:** Camera shake is eliminated, making handheld shooting sharp.

---

### 2. f-number (Aperture)

_Note on terminology: The f-number is an inverse ratio ($f / \text{diameter}$). Therefore, a **larger f-number means a smaller physical opening (aperture area)**, and a **lower f-number means a larger physical opening**._

#### **Larger f-number (e.g., f/11, f/16, f/22) $\rightarrow$ Small Aperture Opening**

- **Exposure:** **Darker image.** The small physical opening lets in less light.
- **Depth of Field (DoF):** **Deep / Large DoF.** Most of the frame from foreground to background remains sharp and in focus (ideal for landscapes, group photos, and architecture).
- **Diffraction:** At very high f-numbers (f/16, f/22), light waves bend around the blade edges, causing **overall optical softness** (loss of micro-contrast).
- **Sunstars:** Point light sources (like streetlights or the sun) turn into distinct starburst/sunstar spikes.

#### **Lower f-number (e.g., f/1.4, f/1.8, f/2.8) $\rightarrow$ Large Aperture Opening**

- **Exposure:** **Brighter image.** The wide opening lets in maximum light, ideal for low-light situations.
- **Depth of Field (DoF):** **Shallow / Narrow DoF.** Only a thin slice of the scene is in focus; the background and foreground become heavily blurred with smooth bokeh (ideal for portraits, macro, and subject isolation).
- **Optical Flaws:** Lenses used wide open may exhibit minor corner vignetting (dark corners) and slight chromatic aberration (color fringing).

---

### 3. ISO Sensitivity

_ISO measures the gain or amplification applied to the signal gathered by the sensor._

#### **Larger / Higher ISO (e.g., ISO 1600, 3200, 6400+)**

- **Exposure:** **Brighter image.** The signal amplification makes the image brighter without needing a slower shutter or wider aperture.
- **Noise / Grain:** Significant digital noise appears (speckling, color artifacts, luminance grain), degrading fine details and texture.
- **Dynamic Range:** **Reduced.** The camera loses the ability to capture extreme contrasts (highlights blow out faster, shadows crush into noise).
- **Color Fidelity:** Colors can look slightly muted, washed out, or show color cast shifts.

#### **Lower ISO (e.g., ISO 50, 100, 200)**

- **Exposure:** **Darker image.** Less signal amplification; requires sufficient ambient light, a larger aperture, or a slower shutter.
- **Noise / Grain:** **Cleanest image.** Minimal to zero visible noise; textures and fine details are sharp.
- **Dynamic Range:** **Maximum.** The sensor captures the widest range of tones from deep shadows to bright highlights.
- **Color Accuracy:** Delivers the most vibrant, rich, and true-to-life colors.

---

### 4. Depth of Field (DoF)

_Depth of Field is the visual outcome produced by your aperture, focal length, and distance to subject._

#### **Larger / Deeper DoF**

- **Sharp Focus Range:** A wide physical zone in front of and behind the focal point is acceptably sharp.
- **Background Appearance:** Background elements remain clear and readable.
- **Separation:** Low subject separation; the subject blends visually into the surroundings (useful for storytelling, context, and landscapes).

#### **Lower / Shallower DoF**

- **Sharp Focus Range:** A narrow, thin plane is in focus; anything slightly in front of or behind the focal point blurs rapidly.
- **Background Appearance:** Background details melt away into soft, out-of-focus blur (bokeh).
- **Separation:** Extreme subject isolation; the viewer's eye is instantly forced onto the sharp subject (essential for portraits, wildlife, and product photography).
