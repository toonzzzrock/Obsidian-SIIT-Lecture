# Question 1: Quantum Computing and Its Computing Architecture
Source: Chapter 00, slides 7-32

## Why questions (Q&A)

**Q1. Why does classical transistor scaling (Moore's law) fail to keep delivering exponential speedup, and why is this used to motivate quantum computing?**
A. Transistor density scaling runs into physical limits around the few-nanometer node: quantum tunneling through gate oxides, heat dissipation per unit area, and lithography cost all stop improving at the historical exponential rate. Classical compute-per-dollar growth is flattening even as some problems (factoring, quantum chemistry simulation, unstructured search) have classical cost that itself grows exponentially in problem size. Quantum architectures do not rely on packing more transistors; they get their exponential resource (the 2^n-dimensional state space of n qubits) from adding qubits linearly, so the motivation is structural, not a continuation of fabrication trends.

**Q2. Why is a quantum algorithm staged as preparation, then computation, then measurement, and why must measurement come last?**
A. Only preparation and computation are unitary and reversible; measurement is a projective, non-unitary operation that collapses superposition and destroys phase relationships between amplitudes. Interference, the mechanism that produces a speedup, needs those phase relationships to survive across the whole computation. Measuring early would throw away exactly the information later gates need to interfere constructively toward the correct answer, so the non-reversible step is deferred to the end (mid-circuit measurement is used only in specific protocols, such as error-correction syndrome extraction, where the measured qubit is not carrying the answer).

**Q3. Why does superposition alone not give a computational advantage without interference?**
A. A uniform superposition over 2^n basis states, if measured immediately, returns one uniformly random n-bit string, no better than a classical random guess. The advantage comes from the gate sequence shaping the amplitudes so that paths leading to wrong answers cancel (destructive interference) and paths leading to the correct answer reinforce (constructive interference) before the final measurement. Superposition supplies the many parallel amplitudes; interference is what turns them into a biased, useful probability distribution.

**Q4. Why does measurement collapse a superposition, and why is this irreversible while unitary computation is reversible?**
A. A projective measurement operator is a rank-reducing projection onto an eigenspace of the observable; many distinct input states can map to the same post-measurement outcome, so the map has no inverse. A unitary gate U satisfies U^-1 = U*, so every unitary step can be undone exactly and no information about the input is lost. Measurement is the one place in the pipeline where information is discarded (turned into a single classical outcome) rather than merely transformed.

**Q5. Why is a quantum computer not just a faster classical computer? Which architectural phenomenon does each pipeline stage rely on?**
A. Preparation typically uses superposition, and often entanglement (via multi-qubit gates such as a controlled-NOT), to spread the state across many basis components at once. Computation uses unitary gates to steer relative phases, maintaining superposition while building up interference patterns. Measurement is where interference becomes observable, as a skewed probability distribution over outcomes rather than a definite classical value. None of these three ingredients has a classical analogue that scales the same way, so raw clock speed is not the relevant comparison.

**Q6. Why do real quantum devices need error correction while the idealized circuit model in the slides does not mention it?**
A. Physical qubits decohere (lose their quantum state to the environment) on a timescale set by T1/T2, and each physical gate has a nonzero error rate (commonly 10^-3 to 10^-2 for current hardware). Without correction, circuit depth is bounded by how many gates can run before accumulated error makes the output useless. Quantum error correction encodes one logical qubit across many physical qubits so that errors can be detected and corrected without collapsing the encoded information. The idealized circuit model in an introductory chapter assumes perfect gates and infinite coherence time, so this overhead layer is out of scope there.

**Q7. Why is quantum parallelism fundamentally different from multi-core classical parallelism?**
A. A multi-core machine runs N independent registers, each holding its own classical value, and produces N separate outputs at once; the resource cost scales linearly with N. A superposition over 2^n basis states is a single register in one exponentially large joint state; there are not 2^n separate physical copies computing independently, and a measurement yields exactly one sample, not 2^n answers. Extracting a useful global property (not just one random sample) requires the interference-based algorithm design described in Q3, not just having many terms present at once.

## Math questions (Q&A)

**Q1. How many real parameters describe a general (normalized, up to global phase) state of n = 3 qubits, versus a classical probability distribution over 3 bits?**
A. A 3-qubit state vector lives in C^8, so it has 8 complex amplitudes, 16 real numbers. Normalization (sum of squared magnitudes = 1) removes one real degree of freedom, and the global phase is physically unobservable, removing one more: 16 - 1 - 1 = 14 real parameters. A classical distribution over 2^3 = 8 outcomes has 8 probabilities constrained to sum to 1, so 8 - 1 = 7 real parameters. The quantum state carries roughly twice as many free real parameters (minus the accounted constraints) as the classical distribution over the same number of basis labels.

**Q2. For n = 10 qubits, compare the number of gates needed to prepare an equal superposition of all basis states with the number of classical function evaluations needed to check every input.**
A. Preparing |+>^⊗10 from |0...0> takes exactly 10 Hadamard gates, one per qubit, since H|0> = |+> and the qubits are independent at that point. A classical algorithm that needs to inspect f(x) for every x in {0,1}^10 needs 2^10 = 1024 evaluations. The gate count for preparation grows linearly (10), while the classical exploration cost grows exponentially (1024). This gap is why preparation is cheap; it says nothing yet about the cost of extracting a useful answer, which depends on the interference structure of the rest of the circuit (Q3 above).

**Q3. Trace the three-stage pipeline for a 2-qubit circuit that starts at |00>, applies H to qubit 1, applies a controlled-NOT (control qubit 1, target qubit 2), and then measures both qubits. Give the state after each stage and the measurement probabilities.**
A. Preparation: start at |00> = (1,0,0,0) in the {|00>,|01>,|10>,|11>} basis.
Computation, step 1 (H on qubit 1): (1/sqrt(2))(|00> + |10>) = (0.7071, 0, 0.7071, 0).
Computation, step 2 (controlled-NOT, control 1 to target 2): (1/sqrt(2))(|00> + |11>) = (0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071), the Bell state.
Measurement: P(00) = |0.7071|^2 = 0.5, P(11) = |0.7071|^2 = 0.5, P(01) = P(10) = 0. Only 00 or 11 can ever be observed, each with probability one half.

**Q4. A qubit is prepared in |+> = (|0> + |1>)/sqrt(2) and measured in the computational basis. Compute the Shannon entropy of the outcome distribution before and after the measurement, and interpret the drop.**
A. Before measurement the outcome distribution is P(0) = P(1) = 1/2, entropy H = -(1/2)log2(1/2) - (1/2)log2(1/2) = 1 bit of uncertainty about the eventual classical outcome. After a single measurement the result is a definite classical bit (say 0), so the post-measurement entropy about that already-obtained outcome is 0 bits. The 1 bit of uncertainty was resolved by the measurement, and the qubit's superposition (which carried more than 1 bit of continuous amplitude/phase information before collapse) is gone; only the 1 classical bit survives.

**Q5. Compare classical linear search and Grover's quantum search for an unsorted list of N = 16 items with one marked item: give the expected number of classical queries and the number of Grover iterations.**
A. Classical unstructured search needs, on average, N/2 = 8 queries to find the marked item (worst case N = 16). Grover's algorithm needs approximately (pi/4) sqrt(N) iterations: (pi/4) sqrt(16) = (pi/4)(4) = pi = about 3.14, so 3 iterations in practice. This is a quadratic reduction (8 versus about 3), a different and smaller gap than the linear-versus-exponential comparison in Q2, which was for full enumeration rather than search for one marked item.

**Q6. (Chapter 00 slide 9's worked example, reproduced.) Convert the decimal integer 30 to its 5-bit binary representation using the slide's method of repeatedly subtracting the closest power of 2.**
A. Closest power of 2 not exceeding 30 is 2^4=16: 30-16=14, digit at 2^4 is 1. Closest power not exceeding 14 is 2^3=8: 14-8=6, digit at 2^3 is 1. Closest power not exceeding 6 is 2^2=4: 6-4=2, digit at 2^2 is 1. Closest power not exceeding 2 is 2^1=2: 2-2=0, digit at 2^1 is 1. Remainder is 0, so digit at 2^0 is 0. Reading the digits from 2^4 down to 2^0: 30 = 1x2^4 + 1x2^3 + 1x2^2 + 1x2^1 + 0x2^0 = 11110 in binary, matching B(30) = 11110_2 from the slide.

**Q7. (Chapter 00 slide 24's worked example, reproduced.) Derive the NOT gate matrix X from its state mapping |0> -> |1>, |1> -> |0> using the outer-product construction, then verify against matrix-vector multiplication on a|0>+b|1>.**
A. X = |1><0| + |0><1| = (0,1)^T(1,0) + (1,0)^T(0,1) = [[0,0],[1,0]] + [[0,1],[0,0]] = [[0,1],[1,0]], exactly the slide's derivation. Verification by matrix multiplication: X(a,b)^T = [[0,1],[1,0]](a,b)^T = (0a+1b, 1a+0b) = (b,a). Verification by linearity on the state mapping: X(a|0>+b|1>) = a(X|0>) + b(X|1>) = a|1> + b|0> = b|0> + a|1>, the same (b,a) result written in Dirac notation, confirming the outer-product matrix and the linear state-mapping expansion agree exactly as the slide's side-by-side comparison shows.
